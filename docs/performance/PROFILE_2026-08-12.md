# First trustworthy performance numbers — 2026-08-12

Build **v1.0-rc3 · `5a42a3f5`**, release export, on the owner's machine:
Intel i7-5700HQ ×8, NVIDIA GeForce GTX 960M (Vulkan 1.4.303), Intel HD 5600
integrated, 60 Hz display, 1280×720.

Every number in this file comes from a report whose `trustworthy` field is
`true`. Everything measured before today was software-rendered — llvmpipe on the
desktop, SwiftShader in the browser — and none of it should be quoted again.

## Desktop (Windows release export)

| scenario | fps mean | p50 frame | p95 frame | worst frame | 1% low |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| title | 60.0 | 16.7 ms | 16.7 ms | 16.7 ms | 60.0 |
| fresh campaign (3 biomes) | 60.0 | 16.7 ms | 16.7 ms | 16.7 ms | 60.0 |
| **endgame save (6 biomes)** | **24.3** | 18.2 ms | **111.5 ms** | **681 ms** | **2.1** |

> **Read the endgame row's tail as a floor.** These were taken before the sampler's
> clock was fixed (see "The sampler's clock was not measuring seconds"), and that bug
> understates long frames specifically. 681 ms is the smallest the worst frame can
> have been, not its size.

Vsync is on (the game enables it on a real GPU), so 60.0 is the display, not the
ceiling. The first two scenarios do not miss a single frame across 1200+ samples.

**The endgame is not slow. It hitches.** Its median frame is 18.2 ms — barely
worse than a fresh game. Its worst is 681 ms: two thirds of a second where the
picture stops. The mean of 24.3 fps describes neither state and should not be
quoted on its own; the pair "p50 55 fps, 1% low 2.1 fps" is the honest summary.

### Where the endgame frame goes

Per-second snapshots from `steady_state` (the full series is in the report):

| | value | reading |
|---|---|---|
| `draw_calls` | 149, flat | the GPU is idle |
| `process_ms` | 80–300 ms | |
| `physics_process_ms` | 11 ms, spiking to 400–627 ms | these spikes ARE the dropped frames |
| `batcher.avg_batch_time_ms` | **285–377 ms** | |
| `batcher.buffer_state` | **`RECOVERY` for the entire run** | it never once caught up |

**A faster GPU would change nothing.** 149 draw calls is a trivial scene; the cost
is CPU-side. Tracked as task #528.

> **Correction, same day — this section originally said "the CPU-side quantum batch
> is the whole cost." That was wrong, and the error was in reading the instrument.**
>
> `avg_batch_time_ms` reports what ONE packet cost. It cannot say what share of the
> session went into the native call — that also needs packets-per-second, which the
> report did not carry. Adding cumulative counters (`native_ms_total`,
> `packets_total`) and re-measuring gave `native call 5.59s of a 24.0s window =
> 23.3% of wall`.
>
> **That 23.3% is also wrong, and the reason is worse — see "The sampler's clock"
> below.** The window it divided by was not seconds. Re-measured on a wall clock:
> **the native call is ~11% of headless wall time**, flat across every packet size.
>
> So the batch is **a quarter** of the cost, not all of it. What makes it *feel* like
> everything is that it arrives in one piece: 18 packets in 24 seconds, each ~310 ms,
> computed synchronously on the main thread. Most frames have no packet and are fine
> — hence the healthy 18.2 ms median — and every frame that catches one stalls for a
> third of a second. **That is the 681 ms worst frame.** The hitch was never the total
> work; it was the lumpiness.
>
> The remaining 76% is not yet attributed, and the number above is from a headless
> debug build, where the mix differs from the shipped one. It is a separate question
> from the hitch, and it is still open.

This is also the first hard evidence about which half of the engine is
expensive. Rendering has been the suspect for months (the 3D field, the emoji
atlas, the metro lines). It is not the renderer.

## How these were taken

Desktop: `scripts/profile-export-runtime.sh` against the Windows export, which
drives it through `powershell.exe`. **WSL can measure a native Windows binary on
the real GPU** — the binary is Windows-native, so nothing is being emulated. It
is Linux binaries and browsers that fall back to software here.

Browser: the kit's `run_web.py` server and injected probe, with the browser
launch swapped for Windows Edge (scaffolding, not part of the kit).

Both instruments were checked against a known-wrong case first: a headless
rehearsal reproduced ~9.9 fps on SwiftShader, agreeing with the old smoke's 9.5
and confirming the two instruments measure the same thing where they overlap.

## The fix, and what it measured (task #528)

Two changes, both verified headless against the endgame save. Neither was reached by
reasoning — the first candidate was wrong and the measurement said so.

**1. Packet size is now bounded by measured time, not by buffer depth alone.**
`BiomeEvolutionBatcher` sized packets on a Fibonacci ladder whose own comment read
"C++ batches are cheap — let fib grow to max and stay there." The decisive experiment
was to vary `SW_MAX_PACKET_STEPS` and watch the *total*. Re-run 2026-08-13 on the
fixed wall clock, and normalised **per phrame-step** — per-*packet* cost cannot
compare two configurations that size their packets differently:

| `SW_MAX_PACKET_STEPS` | packets | ms per step | native share | worst frame |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 230 | 30.20 | 11.4% | 2698 ms |
| 5 | 189 | 34.95 | 11.2% | 2897 ms |
| 21 | 168 | 34.78 | 11.5% | **8082 ms** |

Per-step cost and native share are **flat**: batching buys no meaningful throughput,
it only decides whether the same work arrives as one stall or several frame-sized
slices — and the worst frame triples from cap 1 to cap 21. So the ladder is now
clamped by `PACKET_TIME_BUDGET_MS` divided by the *measured* per-step cost, which
tracks biome count and dimension for free.

Result on the endgame save: **310.5 → 27.3 ms per packet**, worst observed batch
409 → 39 ms, native share unchanged (23.3% → 27.2%). A fresh game did not regress —
1% low actually improved, 15.0 → 20.0 fps.

**2. The integrator no longer collapses its step size near purity.**
`QuantumEvolutionEngine::evolve` chose its substep by solving the purity quadratic for
"time until the trajectory touches Tr(ρ²)=1" and taking 90% of it. That is not a
numerical hazard — a pure state is a physical destination, and `enforce_density_matrix`
already rescales coherences so Tr(ρ²) ≤ 1 after *every* substep. Measured at dim 32
against a 20,000-step reference: the old rule took **167 substeps and 318 ms** for one
0.1 s phrame at Tr(ρ²)=0.999, and was **no more accurate than its own single-substep
case** — both ≈1e-3. The substeps bought nothing.

Replaced with RK4 under a relative-change bound and a hard `MAX_SUBSTEPS` ceiling: the
same phrame is now one substep at ≈1e-7 error — cheaper *and* four orders more
accurate — and no state a player can build can make one phrame cost unbounded work.
`tests/test_evolve_parity.gd` passes 39/39, including the trace and purity bounds.

**The cost this trades, recorded so it is not a surprise later.** RK4 evaluates the
generator four times per substep where Euler evaluated it once. On the common path —
a low-purity state that the old controller cleared in a single Euler step — the new
one still takes a single substep (that is what `REL_CHANGE_PER_SUBSTEP = 0.1` was
chosen for) but pays 4 RHS evaluations for it. Measured in the actual game, that cost
did not show: a fresh boot went 29.19 → 29.43 fps, batch median 17.44 → 17.05 ms,
1% low 15 → 20. It is invisible because biome dimensions are small (≤64) and `evolve`
is not the dominant term in a packet.

It is **dimension-sensitive, and the ceiling is not hypothetical.** The sentence that
stood here said "if biome dimension ever grows past 64 — re-measure." That was wrong
about where the game already is. `economy_variables.max_biome_qubits = 6`, i.e. **dim
64 is the configured ceiling and a player reaches it by injecting icons**; the
profiled `endrun_ending` save already has Village sitting at 6 qubits, with the other
five at 1, 2, 4, 4 and 5.

Measured 2026-08-13 by minting an all-6-qubit variant of that save through the rig
(canonical save/load, no hand-edited `.tres`) and A/B-ing the two integrators headless
at 60 s each, normalised per phrame-step:

| save | RK4 (shipped) | old Euler | |
|---|---|---|---|
| `endrun_ending` as shipped | **34.25 ms/step** | 52.15 ms/step | RK4 **1.52× cheaper** |
| all six biomes at 6 qubits | 82.71 ms/step | **44.87 ms/step** | RK4 **1.84× dearer** |

Both directions are real and both are expected: RK4 wins wherever the old controller
hits its substep cliff, and loses at uniform dim 64 where the old rule takes one Euler
step and RK4 pays four generator evaluations for its one. The lab's ">10×" was the
cost of `evolve` alone under dense random Lindblad operators; in the game, where L is
sparse and `evolve` is one term in a packet, the ceiling penalty is **1.84×**, not 10×.

Consequence worth stating plainly: at that ceiling one phrame of six dim-64 biomes
costs ~83 ms, so **`PACKET_TIME_BUDGET_MS = 25` cannot be honoured** — the budget's
floor is one step and a step cannot be subdivided. It is over budget with the old
integrator too (~45 ms). `REL_CHANGE_PER_SUBSTEP` is the dial if that trade needs
revisiting; the structural answer is to move the packet off the main thread
(`MultiBiomeLookaheadEngine::submit_lookahead_job` already runs one on a real thread,
and the batcher's hot path does not use it). Not done — it is a change to the
batcher's state machine, not a tuning pass.

## The sampler's clock was not measuring seconds

Found 2026-08-13 while trying to reconcile two runs of the table above that disagreed
by 2.7×, one of which reported the native call as **117% of its own window**. A share
above 100% is impossible for a synchronous main-thread call, so the instrument was
wrong, not the game.

`PerfSampler` accumulated `_process(delta)`. Godot multiplies that delta by
`Engine.time_scale` and clamps it to stop the spiral of death, so under load it is not
wall time. Checked directly against a stopwatch: **the run took 280.7 s and the report
said 32.4 s.** `Engine.time_scale` was 1.0 throughout, so the whole gap was the clamp.

Two consequences, both of which touch numbers already published:

- **Every "share of wall" figure computed from that clock was inflated**, including
  the 23.3% correction above. Re-measured honestly: **~11%**.
- **The frame-time tail was understated by roughly 12×.** The same run that reported
  `worst frame 884 ms` reports **10,384 ms** on a wall clock. Frame *medians* barely
  move; it is the stalls — the thing the whole report exists to find — that were
  hidden.

`PerfSampler` now takes its window and every frame time from `Time.get_ticks_usec()`,
reports `clock: "wall"` plus `engine_time_scale_min/mean` so a dilated run is legible
as dilated, and `tests/test_perf_lane.py` pins it. The batcher additionally exports
`native_ms_per_step` / `steps_total`, because per-packet cost cannot compare two builds
once packet sizing is adaptive.

**The desktop table at the top of this file was taken with the broken clock.** Its
p50s are close to right; its `worst frame 681 ms` and `1% low 2.1` are floors, and the
real stall was likely several times larger. The Windows re-measurement is what settles
it, and the kit needs a rebuilt exe before it can.

Honest accounting: this second change is worth having on its own merits, but it was
**not** what fixed the hitch. It moved the endgame batch 356 → 331 ms. The packet
budget did the rest. The synthetic benchmark that motivated it mispredicted the real
save, which is why the fix was measured in the game before being believed.
